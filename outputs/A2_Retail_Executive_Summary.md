**Scope.** This analysis examined 260 unique transactions from 01 January 2025 to 29 June 2025. The workbook states that records are simulated for teaching. They do not represent actual customers or retailer operations, so the results are an exercise in reproducible analysis rather than a basis for staffing, inventory, pricing or regional investment decisions. Revenue is reported in Kenyan shillings (KES). I removed exact repeated transaction IDs, kept unusual transactions, recorded missingness, used an explicit Unknown category for missing region/payment method and imputed missing unit prices using the median within product category. Net revenue was recalculated as units multiplied by unit price and one minus the discount rate; differences from the supplied revenue were retained in the audit fields.

**Overall performance in this sample.** The cleaned records sum to KES 8,813,868.92 across 1,296 units. Mean transaction revenue is KES 33,899.50, while the median is KES 8,964.20. The mean being higher than the median indicates that a smaller number of large transactions pull the average upward. The reported totals depend on the assignment’s revenue formula and the price imputation described above. An unusually high unit price was retained and flagged rather than removed: a high value might be a genuine transaction or a data-entry error and the workbook alone cannot resolve that question. A manager should verify such observations against original receipts before making decisions.

**Category and time findings.** Electronics generated the greatest total revenue, KES 5,336,804.39, from 40 transactions. Clothing had the largest number of units sold (302). Revenue and unit rankings can differ because products have different prices and discount levels; unit volume is not the same as financial contribution. In the observed months, 2025-02 had the highest total revenue (KES 2,511,100.09) and 2025-03 the lowest (KES 852,130.58). These are descriptions of this short January–June 2025 sample, not evidence of a stable seasonal pattern. A full year or multiple years are needed to distinguish seasonality from random variation.

**Channel and customer-spend context.** Store had the largest aggregate revenue (KES 4,607,852.36) among the listed channels. Aggregate revenue depends on both transaction count and average transaction value; it should not be interpreted as evidence that customers in one channel spend more until both measures and the group sizes are considered. Payment-method and regional differences may also reflect missing classifications, transaction mix, availability or sampling rather than underlying customer preferences. No causal conclusion is supported.

**Model results and their limits.** The Linear regression had held-out MAE KES 23,151.35, RMSE KES 35,013.69 and R-squared 0.968. MAE is the average absolute error; RMSE penalizes large errors more; R-squared describes variation explained relative to a mean prediction on this test split. Because units, price and discount directly determine the target, strong performance mainly demonstrates the arithmetic relationship and should not be presented as a demand forecast. The random split also does not test performance on a later period. A future forecasting study should define the forecast horizon, use a chronological split and use only information available at prediction time.

**Recommendations.** First, validate extreme prices and the revenue inconsistencies against source receipts, while preserving an audit trail; do not automatically discard high values. Second, collect a full year or more of verified transactions, stock availability, product identifiers and promotion context before evaluating inventory or sales strategies. A particularly useful additional field is **inventory availability at transaction time**, since recorded sales can be constrained by stockouts. Missing values, imputation, extreme observations, short coverage and synthetic records all reduce confidence. Limit access to transaction-level files, collect only fields needed for analysis and avoid using incomplete regional or payment comparisons to treat customers differently. Apply appropriate access controls, retention limits and privacy review to any real customer data.
